02—Welcome

How to Use This Deck

This is a practical operating manual for thesis and dissertation students.

Use it in layers
  • Before starting research: focus on mindset, literature, and advisor meetings.
  • While running experiments: focus on notebooks, reproducibility, and diagnosis.
  • While writing: focus on structure, figures, claims, and revision cycles.
  • Before a proposal, defense, or conference talk: focus on story and practice.
Scope of the advice

Most of the feedback in this deck applies to all thesis and dissertation students. Some examples reflect my own area — computer vision, signal processing, and machine learning — but the underlying ideas carry across all ECE research areas.

A note on sources

04—Research Mindset

Research Mindset

The hardest transition is not technical. It is moving from solving assigned problems to finding, shaping, and defending original ones.

05—Research Mindset

From Structured Problems to Open Ones

Coursework is usually designed so the answer exists. Research is designed around uncertainty.

In coursework

The question is framed, the assumptions are known, and feedback arrives quickly.

In research

The question may be wrong, the assumptions may fail, and feedback can be noisy or delayed.

Your new job

Make uncertainty smaller through deliberate experiments and clear communication.

Core shift: no one tells you the right answer. Often, no one knows yet whether there is a right answer.
06—Research Mindset

Slow Down to Speed Up

“Slow down to speed up.”

  • Verify assumptions before building on them.
  • Change one thing at a time so the outcome is interpretable.
  • Run the smallest experiment that can answer the next question.
  • Keep the trail clear enough that your future self can reconstruct it.

Methodical work feels slower in the moment; it usually wins over weeks and semesters.

07—Research Mindset

Never Say “It Doesn’t Work”

That sentence gives your advisor no diagnostic information.

Weak report

“I tried the method and it does not work.”

The failure could be in the idea, code, data, assumptions, metric, implementation, or interpretation.
Strong report

“I narrowed the failure to step B. Step A matches the expected output on test input X. At B, the output has failure mode Y. I ruled out W and Z.”

Diagnosis is part of the research contribution.
08—Research Mindset

Be the Motor and the Rudder

Motor: work hard

By graduate school, everyone is smart. The people who pull ahead usually show sustained effort, not occasional brilliance.

Rudder: steer

Periodically ask whether you are working on the right problem, with the right experiments, at the right level of abstraction.

  • ✓Protect time for deep work.
  • ✓Protect time for health, relationships, and recovery.
  • ✓Do not confuse being busy with making progress.
  • ✓Every few weeks, ask: what result would change my plan?
09—Research Mindset

Toy Models: Start with the Smallest Version

Find the simplest toy model that captures the main idea.

  1. Strip the problem down
    Remove all realism that is not needed for the core ambiguity or mechanism.
  2. Make predictions
    Ask what each proposed method should do in this toy setting.
  3. Run and reason
    If it fails here, scaling up will not rescue it. If it works here, you have intuition.
  4. Scale carefully
    Add one source of realism at a time: noise, dimension, data size, hardware, people, or constraints.
10—Research Mindset

The ε-Ball Around the Advisor’s Suggestion

Doing exactly what was suggested is often not enough. The research move is to explore nearby variants.

Strong student pattern: try the suggested approach, observe why it does not quite work, test a small neighborhood of variants, and return with the best explanation and next step.
  • What if the loss changes?
  • What if the input is simplified?
  • What if one assumption is made explicit?
  • What if the method is tested on a case where it must succeed?
  • What if it is tested on a case where it must fail?
11—Research Mindset

Know the Literature; Build a Reputation

Literature discipline
  • Track the major lines of work, not just one paper.
  • For each paper, record the problem, method, claim, evidence, and limitation.
  • Look for what everyone assumes but no one has tested.
  • Do not be caught missing closely related work.
Reputation discipline
  • Be the person who gives careful updates.
  • Be the person whose figures and code can be trusted.
  • Be generous in collaboration and credit.
  • Every talk, paper, email, and meeting is a data point.
12—Research Mindset

Research Mindset Self-Check

Can I state the current research question in one sentence?
Can I name the smallest experiment that tests the next assumption?
Can I explain where the current failure occurs?
Have I changed only one thing since the last interpretable result?
Do I know the closest related papers?
Am I working hard and steering strategically?
13—Research Process

Research Process

A thesis is built through repeated cycles: question, literature, hypothesis, experiment, diagnosis, explanation, writing.

14—Research Process

Choose a Problem You Can Actually Move

A good graduate research problem is important enough to matter and bounded enough to attack.

  1. Problem
    What is the gap, limitation, ambiguity, or engineering need?
  2. Stakeholders
    Who cares if this is solved: researchers, practitioners, users, or society?
  3. Leverage
    What tool, dataset, theory, device, or insight gives you an angle?
  4. Evidence
    What result would convince a skeptical reader that progress was made?
15—Research Process

Build a Literature Map

Do not just collect PDFs. Convert reading into a map of ideas, assumptions, methods, datasets, and open problems.

Canon

The foundational papers everyone cites and expects you to know.

Frontier

The most recent papers that define what is currently possible.

Gap

The limitation, mismatch, or untested assumption where your work enters.

Reading is not separate from research; it is how you learn what question is worth asking.

16—Research Process

Make Advisor Meetings Useful

A good meeting is not a status confession. It is a decision-making session.

  • ✓Send a concise agenda or update before the meeting when appropriate.
  • ✓Bring one or two figures, tables, or diagnostics—not a verbal blur.
  • ✓State what changed since last time and what you learned.
  • ✓End with explicit next actions, owners, and dates.
  • ✓Keep a running decision log so old questions do not reopen endlessly.
17—Research Process

Design Experiments That Answer Questions

Every experiment should have a claim it could support or refute.

  1. Question
    What uncertainty is this experiment reducing?
  2. Prediction
    What should happen if the hypothesis is right?
  3. Controls
    What baseline, ablation, or sanity check is needed?
  1. Metric
    What will be measured, and why is it meaningful?
  2. Failure mode
    What result would make you change your mind?
  3. Record
    Where will the config, data, code, and output be stored?
18—Research Process

Keep a Research Notebook

Your notebook is the memory of the project.

  • Record the date, goal, hypothesis, setup, parameters, commits, datasets, and outcomes.
  • Paste or link figures and tables, not just prose descriptions.
  • Write one sentence after each experiment: “What did I learn?”
  • Record dead ends; they often become limitations, ablations, or future work.
  • Use a system you will actually maintain: paper, Markdown, OneNote, Overleaf, Notion, lab wiki, or a hybrid.
The best notebook lets someone else reproduce the result—and lets you reproduce it six months later.
19—Research Process

Version Control, Backups, and Naming

Catastrophic data loss is preventable. A thesis project needs a storage plan before it needs a final result.
  • ✓Use Git or an equivalent version-control system for code and writing when possible.
  • ✓Separate raw data, processed data, scripts, results, and figures.
  • ✓Use names that survive time: dates, dataset, method, metric, version.
  • ✓Back up to at least two places, with one not on your laptop.
  • ✓Never overwrite the only copy of a result used in a paper, thesis, or defense slide.
20—Research Process

Data, Code, and Reproducibility

Reproducibility is not an afterthought; it is how you protect your own progress.

Data

Document source, permissions, preprocessing, splits, exclusions, and derived versions.

Code

Record environment, dependencies, scripts, configuration, random seeds, and commit hashes.

Results

Connect every figure and table to the exact run that produced it.

  • Treat “I can reproduce this figure” as a milestone.
  • Automate routine plots and tables when possible.
  • Keep a README for every major project folder.
21—Research Process

Negative Results Are Information

A clean negative result is better than an ambiguous positive result.

  1. Define the expectation
    What should have happened if the idea were correct?
  2. Check the implementation
    Can the code solve a case where the answer is known?
  3. Reduce the setting
    Does the problem persist in a toy model?
  4. Extract the lesson
    Is the failure due to data, assumptions, scale, metric, or theory?

Negative results become good thesis material when they are diagnostic and well explained.

22—Research Process

A Healthy Research Rhythm

Daily

Make one concrete movement: read, implement, test, write, analyze, or plan.

Weekly

Summarize what changed, what you learned, what is blocked, and the next experiment.

Monthly

Step back: is the problem still right, and is the evidence accumulating toward a thesis?

Working rule: if a week passes and there is no artifact—figure, paragraph, result, note, code, or decision—the project is probably drifting.
23—Thesis / Dissertation Path

Thesis & Dissertation Path

The degree document is not just a long report. It is the formal argument that your research contribution is adequate in scope, quality, and originality.

24—Thesis / Dissertation Path

What the Thesis or Dissertation Must Prove

At the end, the committee is judging a body of work and your command of it.

  • You understand the relevant literature and can situate your contribution.
  • You can define a meaningful problem and justify why it matters.
  • You can design and execute credible research methods.
  • You can interpret evidence honestly, including limitations.
  • You can communicate the work in writing and in an oral defense.
For Ph.D. students, the standard is a dissertation-scale original contribution. For M.S. thesis students, the scope is smaller, but the need for clear evidence and writing is the same.
25—Thesis / Dissertation Path

Committee and Milestones

Your advisor guides the research; your committee certifies the final work.

  1. Advisor alignment
    Clarify expectations for meetings, authorship, publications, funding, and timelines.
  2. Committee formation
    Follow department/college rules and form the committee early enough to be useful.
  3. Proposal or prospectus
    Convert an idea into a defensible plan with scope, literature, methods, and expected contributions.
  4. Defense
    Present the completed work, answer questions, and make required revisions.
  5. Final submission
    Complete college and Graduate School formatting, approval, and ETD steps.
26—Thesis / Dissertation Path

Proposal: Turn a Topic into a Plan

A proposal should reduce risk before the final semester.

  • Problem statement
  • Motivation and significance
  • Related work and gap
  • Preliminary results
  • Research aims or questions
  • Methods and evaluation plan
  • Risks and alternatives
  • Timeline and milestones
  • Expected contributions
  • Publication or dissemination plan
Good proposal question: What evidence would make the committee believe this project is feasible and worth completing?
27—Thesis / Dissertation Path

Anatomy of the Document

Exact structure varies by field and advisor, but the argument is usually stable.

  1. Introduction
    Problem, motivation, gap, thesis statement, and contributions.
  2. Background / Related Work
    What the reader needs to understand the field and your position in it.
  3. Methods / System / Theory
    The technical contribution, written so a qualified reader can reproduce or evaluate it.
  4. Results
    Evidence, comparisons, ablations, error analysis, and interpretation.
  5. Discussion / Conclusion
    What changed because of the work, limitations, and credible future directions.
28—Thesis / Dissertation Path

Chapter-by-Chapter Pipeline

Do not wait until “all the research is done” to start the thesis.

  • ✓Start a living document early with headings and placeholders.
  • ✓Write the introduction repeatedly as your understanding improves.
  • ✓Turn each mature result into a thesis-ready figure, caption, and paragraph.
  • ✓Use papers, reports, and proposal text as draft material, but integrate them into one argument.
  • ✓Keep a “missing evidence” list for claims that still need support.
29—Thesis / Dissertation Path

Defense Preparation

The defense is not a mystery exam. It is a formal conversation about work you have lived with for months or years.
  • Confirm committee, date, room/Zoom logistics, forms, and deadlines early.
  • Send the manuscript with enough time for serious reading.
  • Practice the talk aloud; do at least one full mock defense.
  • Prepare backup slides for details, derivations, extra results, and likely questions.
  • Open by making the problem and contribution unmistakable. Close by making completion unmistakable.
30—Thesis / Dissertation Path

ETD and Submission Resources

Formatting and submission are part of graduation. Treat them as milestones, not clerical afterthoughts.

  • UH Graduate School ETD informationTemplates, approval forms, accessibility requirements, Vireo submission, embargo, copyright, and FAQ.
  • Cullen College thesis/dissertation guideCollege-level steps for defense, revision, approval form, formatting review, and electronic submission.

Use official UH, Cullen College, and department pages for current deadlines and forms. Procedures can change.
31—Writing

Writing

Writing is not packaging after the research. It is how you clarify what the research means.

32—Writing

Why Writing Matters

Only the good papers count.

A thesis or dissertation is evaluated through writing. Even excellent experiments lose force if the reader cannot see the problem, contribution, evidence, and limitations.

  • Write to make the work easy to understand, easy to trust, and hard to misread.
  • A strong paper or chapter can shape your reputation for years.
  • A weakly written document makes even good research look uncertain.
33—Writing

The Canonical Research Story

Most research writing can be organized as a simple sequence.

  1. Problem
    What are you addressing, and why should the reader care?
  2. Gap
    What do existing solutions do, and why are they not enough?
  3. Idea
    What is your approach, and what is the key insight?
  4. Evidence
    What experiments, analyses, or proofs support the claims?
  5. Change
    What does the reader now know or do differently because of your work?
34—Writing

Write a Dynamite Introduction

The introduction is the most-read part of the document. It must do real work.

What problem am I solving?
Why is the problem important now?
Why are existing approaches insufficient?
What is the core idea?
What exactly is new?
What evidence supports the contribution?
A good introduction lets a busy reader understand the whole paper or chapter before the details begin.
35—Writing

One Figure Should Carry the Main Idea

Aim for a visual abstract: one figure and caption that reveal the contribution.

  • Show the problem setup.
  • Show the key mechanism or system.
  • Show the contrast with prior work.
  • Show what the reader should notice.
  • Caption should be self-contained.
  • Labels should be readable without zooming.
  • Use consistent notation and colors.
  • Avoid decorative complexity.

If a reader sees only one figure, which one should it be?

36—Writing

Results: Claims Need Evidence

Every major claim should connect to a result, comparison, ablation, proof, or diagnostic.

  1. Main result
    Does the proposed method/system/theory work on the target problem?
  2. Baseline
    How does it compare to the most relevant existing approach or simple alternative?
  3. Ablation
    Which components are necessary, and what happens when they are removed?
  4. Error analysis
    Where does it fail, and what does that teach us?
  5. Interpretation
    What conclusion is justified—and what conclusion is not?
37—Writing

Use Fewer Words

Before

“The underlying assumption of this work is that the estimate of a given node will only depend on nodes within a patch...”

After

“We assume local influence: nodes depend only on other nodes within a patch.”

  • Cut throat-clearing phrases.
  • Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs.
  • One paragraph should make one move.
  • Delete sentences that do not change the reader’s understanding.
  • Concise writing is not shorter thinking; it is clearer thinking.
38—Writing

Equations, Figures, and Captions

Equations

Introduce with words. Define symbols. Refer by meaning, not only number.

Figures

Make them readable, consistent, and tightly connected to the text.

Captions

Tell the reader what to notice and why it matters.

Skimmers should still understand the story from title, abstract, figures, captions, and section headings.
39—Writing

Revision Workflow

Do not revise everything at once. Revise in passes.

  1. Argument pass
    Does the structure make a coherent claim?
  2. Evidence pass
    Is every claim supported by the right figure, table, proof, or citation?
  3. Reader pass
    Where will a skeptical but fair reader get lost?
  4. Conciseness pass
    Can any sentence, paragraph, figure, or section be cut?
  5. Mechanics pass
    References, notation, formatting, cross-references, accessibility, and PDF checks.
40—Ethics, Collaboration & Talks

Ethics, Collaboration & Talks

Research is a trust system. Your data, authorship, citations, code, and presentations all build—or damage—that trust.

41—Ethics, Collaboration & Talks

Collaborate Generously; Credit Carefully

Science is a team sport, but credit must be explicit and fair.

  • Discuss authorship expectations early and revisit when contributions change.
  • Include people who made substantive intellectual, experimental, coding, or writing contributions.
  • Cite related work accurately, even when it competes with yours.
  • Do not disparage prior work to make yours look stronger.
  • A generous, precise tone builds long-term credibility.
42—Ethics, Collaboration & Talks

Research Integrity

Rule of thumb: the reader should be able to trust that your figure, table, image, quotation, and citation mean exactly what you say they mean.
  • ✓Do not cherry-pick results without saying what was excluded.
  • ✓Figures and images must faithfully represent the underlying data.
  • ✓Do not hide failed baselines or inconvenient limitations.
  • ✓Do not reuse text, figures, or data without permission and citation.
  • ✓Use AI tools only in ways consistent with advisor, course, journal, conference, and university policies.
43—Ethics, Collaboration & Talks

Talks Are Research Artifacts

A talk should not be a compressed thesis. It should be a guided story.

Problem

What did you address, and why does it matter?

Key idea

What makes your approach different or insightful?

Evidence

How well did it work, and what should the audience remember?

  • Use verbal headings so distracted listeners can re-enter.
  • Ask questions or set up competing hypotheses when possible.
  • Practice the opening and transitions until they are smooth.
44—Ethics, Collaboration & Talks

Short Talk Checklist

For a 3–10 minute talk, advertise the work; do not unload every detail.

What problem did I address?
Why is it interesting?
Why is it hard?
What was the key idea?
How well did it work?
What should people ask me afterward?
For any short talk, there is no excuse not to practice aloud, start to finish, multiple times.
45—Ethics, Collaboration & Talks

End Deliberately

Do not let the ending dissolve into “Any questions?”

  • Remind the audience what you promised at the start.
  • State the contribution one last time.
  • Thank collaborators, funding sources, and the audience as appropriate.
  • Then clearly signal the transition to questions.
  • Keep backup slides ready for likely technical questions.

A confident ending helps the audience understand that the argument is complete.

46—Resources & Routines

Resources & Routines

The most successful students build systems: for reading, writing, experiments, meetings, backups, and deadlines.

47—Resources & Routines

Build Your Writing Infrastructure Early

Do not wait until the final semester to learn the tools that will hold your thesis or dissertation together.

Writing system

Use LaTeX, Word, or another approved workflow consistently. The key is stable formatting, cross-references, and version history.

Reference system

Use a bibliography manager from the beginning so citations, PDFs, notes, and BibTeX entries do not become a last-minute archaeology project.

Project system

Keep manuscript, figures, tables, code, data notes, and results organized so every claim can be traced back to evidence.

The tool choice matters less than the habit: make the work reproducible, searchable, and easy to revise.

48—Resources & Routines

LaTeX: Use It as a Research Tool

LaTeX is not just for equations; it is a system for maintaining a large technical argument.

  1. Start from the right template
    Use the department, college, journal, or conference template before heavy writing begins.
  2. Write modularly
    Separate chapters or sections into files; keep figures and tables in predictable folders.
  3. Use labels, not manual numbers
    Cross-reference sections, equations, figures, tables, and algorithms with labels so the document can change safely.
  4. Compile often
    Fix warnings early, especially missing references, overfull boxes, broken citations, and figure path errors.
49—Resources & Routines

Bibliography Managers: Citation Hygiene

Your references are part of the research record, not decoration at the end.

  • Choose one managerZotero, Mendeley, EndNote, BibDesk, or another system is fine; switching repeatedly creates errors.
  • Clean the metadataCheck author names, title capitalization, venue, year, DOI, arXiv version, and duplicate entries before citing.
  • Keep notes with papersRecord the contribution, limitation, dataset, method, and why the paper matters to your work.
  • Cite generously and preciselyDo not cite a paper you have not read closely enough to explain; do not omit closely related work.
50—Resources & Routines

Figures, Tables, and Reproducible Builds

A figure in the thesis should not be a mysterious file on someone's laptop.

Can I identify the script, notebook, data version, and parameters that produced this figure?
Are axis labels, units, legends, fonts, and captions readable without zooming?
Are the same colors, names, metrics, and baselines used consistently across chapters?
Can I regenerate the table or figure after a reviewer, advisor, or committee member requests a change?
Have I preserved the underlying data and avoided manual edits that obscure the provenance?
Does the caption say what the reader should notice and what conclusion is justified?
Good practice: keep generated figures separate from source data, and document the command or workflow that produced each final artifact.

52—Resources & Routines

Your Weekly Operating System

A simple weekly update can prevent months of drift.

  1. What changed?
    Results, readings, code, writing, decisions, meetings, or obstacles since last week.
  2. What did I learn?
    The specific insight, not just the activity completed.
  3. What is blocked?
    Data, equipment, permissions, code, theory, writing, scheduling, or feedback.
  4. What is next?
    One to three concrete actions that can be checked by next week.
53—Resources & Routines

Combined Big Picture

Research, writing, and speaking are three forms of the same discipline: clear thinking communicated effectively.

  • ✓Research develops the idea.
  • ✓Writing makes the idea legible.
  • ✓Talks make the idea memorable.
  • ✓Ethics and reproducibility make the idea trustworthy.
  • ✓Routines keep the idea moving when motivation fluctuates.
54—Resources & Routines

Final Self-Check

I can state my research question and why it matters.
I have a literature map, not just a folder of PDFs.
I keep a notebook that records experiments and lessons.
I can reproduce my important figures and tables.
I know my next milestone and the forms/deadlines attached to it.
I am writing before the final semester, not after the work is “done.”
I can explain my contribution to a technical outsider.
I am building a reputation for careful, generous, trustworthy work.
